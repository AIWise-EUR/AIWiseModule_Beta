How Do We Use AI?

Section 1 · Prompt

Designing the input

A prompt is a text input given to AI to instruct a task. Research defines prompt engineering as "the skill of articulating a problem, its context, and the constraints of the desired solution to an AI assistant" (Federiakin et al., 2024).

In this module, prompting is calibration. It is the act of defining what you want to do, what you expect, what process to follow, and under what conditions. Think of it as designing a virtual workspace where AI operates on your terms. To write a good prompt, you need to understand GenAI, but you also need to understand yourself: your task, your intention, your current stage, and your boundaries.

"Learning how to interact with AI is not unlike being someone who's really good at asking questions. Prompting AI is very similar. You can't just randomly ask a bunch of questions. Asking AI to be an assistant to you requires some expertise and artistry of how to prompt it."

— Jensen Huang, CEO of NVIDIA (Cleo Abram, "Huge Conversations", January 2025)

Six types of information AI uses to process a task

Research consistently shows that AI performs significantly better when certain types of information are clearly provided. The following six are the key components that support how AI processes and responds to your request.

Task Specification
Clear Instruction
Information about what needs to be done. The more you specify the purpose, scope, context, and conditions of a task, the narrower AI's working space becomes.
If you don't set it: AI interprets the task in the most generic way possible. Whether it matches your intent is left to chance.
Consensus: treated as baseline across all major prompt engineering surveys
Perspective Assignment
Role / Persona
Information about what perspective AI should operate from. Setting a role is not a technique to boost AI accuracy — it is the act of deciding what position you want AI to occupy for this task.
If you don't set it: AI operates as a generic "helpful assistant."
Evidence: no measurable accuracy improvement on benchmarks (Zheng et al., 2024 EMNLP; 162 personas, 4 LLM families). Effective as a tone and framing tool.
Structure of a Task
Task Decomposition
Information about the structure of a task. When you throw a complex task at AI in one piece, you cannot tell which part of the output went wrong.
If you don't set it: AI decomposes the task on its own, but you cannot know if its decomposition matches your intent.
Evidence: Least-to-Most prompting showed 83.5pp gain on compositional benchmarks (Zhou et al., 2022 ICLR)
Desired Output
Few-shot Examples
Information about what the desired output looks like. The act of creating an example forces you to check: do I actually know what I want? Modern models can infer intent without examples, but that means the model interprets on its own.
If you don't set it: AI responds in its most generic form. Without a standard, you cannot judge if it is right.
Evidence: established in Brown et al. (2020, NeurIPS) GPT-3 paper. Few-shot CoT scored highest in Schulhoff et al. benchmarks.
Reasoning Steps
Chain-of-Thought
Information about the reasoning process. Asking AI to show its intermediate steps opens a window for you to check its logic.
If you don't set it: You get only the conclusion, with no visibility into how it got there. If it sounds plausible, you just accept it.
Evidence: Wei et al. (2022, NeurIPS) GSM8K 18% → 58%. However, effects are limited outside math/symbolic reasoning (Sprague et al., 2024).
Shape of the Output
Output Format
Information about the shape of the output. The same content delivered as a table, a list, a single sentence, or a set of questions changes how you can use it afterward. Specifying the format is not a convenience — it is designing how the output will integrate into your workflow.
If you don't set it: AI defaults to its standard form (usually long paragraphs).
Revisited in Section 3 (Integrate) from the integration strategy perspective.
Proactive GenAI Usage

If you think through these components before prompting, you stay in control of the task. If you skip them, AI fills them in for you, and because its output looks plausible, you may not realize decisions were made without you. Having your own ideas about these components before you let AI work is what we call proactive GenAI usage.

Section 2 · Critique

Output is not an answer — it is a candidate

What AI gives you is not a final answer but a candidate: candidate structure, candidate wording, candidate comparison, candidate interpretation. Output is material to review, not material to adopt.

This connects directly to what you learned in C1. GenAI output is statistically likely, not factually verified. It is conditioned on prior tokens, not on reasoning. It is bounded by training data. It reflects a distribution, not authorship. Without your domain knowledge, critique is impossible.

Read — Identify what came out
  • Is this a response to what I requested?
  • What structure did AI use? (list, paragraphs, table, etc.)
  • What overall claim or direction is it taking?
Example
Student asked "Extract the common themes from these three papers." AI returned three paragraphs. Observation: AI identified three themes, assigned papers to each, and wrote in paragraph form.
Question — Generate questions before judging
  • Is this content factually verifiable?
  • Is anything missing?
  • Are any parts overly general or vague?
  • Does the output include claims I can't verify?
  • Did the output stay within my requested scope?
Example
Are these three themes actually from the papers, or did AI generalize? Is the third theme too broad? Paper B is assigned to two themes — is that accurate? I only asked for common themes, but AI added "implications" — did it exceed my request scope?
Verify — Judge against criteria
  • If facts, citations, or numbers are included — can the source be verified?
  • Does the output match the scope of my request?
  • Can I explain this content myself?
  • Does this output reflect my intent and context?
Example
Checking Paper A: AI's first theme is correct but was a secondary point, not the core finding. The third theme uses phrasing that doesn't appear in any original paper — AI generalized. The "implications" section covers a stage I haven't reached yet.
Decide — Select, modify, or discard
  • Which parts can I use and which should I discard?
  • Which parts need modification?
  • Is a re-prompt necessary?
Example
Themes 1 and 2 are usable as starting points. Theme 3 is discarded. The implications section is deleted. Theme 2's paper assignment needs verification — may re-prompt after checking the original.
Section 3 · Integrate

How AI output enters your work

Integration does not start after you receive the output — it starts at the prompting stage. The moment you decide what format to receive the output in, you have already determined how safe and controllable the integration will be.

"Compare these papers" produces paragraphs that easily bleed into your writing. "Make a comparison table" keeps the output separate from your text, verifiable cell by cell. Choosing the output format is not a convenience — it is a safety mechanism for integration.

Requested FormatIntegration Characteristics
Comparison tableVerifiable and extractable cell by cell. Does not blend into your prose.
Question listServes as a thinking prompt. No copy-paste risk. You have to write the answers.
Single sentence summaryA single claim — easy to accept or reject clearly.
Keyword / term listEasy to selectively adopt. You write the sentences.
Bullet point outlineUse the structure as reference; fill in the content yourself.
Full paragraphHighest risk of blending into your writing. Use with caution.

Segment — Break output into units

Break the output into units that can be judged independently. Rows for tables, items for lists, claims for paragraphs.

Tag — Adopt / Modify / Discard

Assign a tag to each unit:

  • Adopt  accurate, matches my intent, ready to use
  • Modify  direction is right, needs adjustment
  • Discard  inaccurate, outside scope, or I cannot verify it
Execute — Act on each tag
  • Adopt → place in your work; confirm source and context.
  • Modify → define what to change; return to original source or re-prompt.
  • Discard → delete; record why in one line.
Review — Look at the whole

After integration, look at the whole:

  • Does this reflect my thinking?
  • Has AI's language remained unchanged in my text?
  • Can I explain and defend every part?
Human Accountability Checklist
  • Are the core ideas and judgments in this work my own?
  • Have I verified all AI-generated content that I adopted?
  • Can I explain and defend every part of this work?
  • Can I transparently disclose how AI was used?
Based on the Author Checklist from Cheng, Calhoun, & Reedy (2025). Advances in Simulation, 10, 22.
Closing · Template

Building your prompt

AI processes structured input more accurately than prose paragraphs. Formatting changes alone can shift accuracy by up to 76 percentage points (Sclar et al., ICLR 2024). Two common structuring formats are Markdown and XML.

Markdown
XML

Markdown uses # for headings and - for lists — intuitive and easy for humans to read. XML uses explicit opening and closing tags, which models parse more precisely. XML is the only delimiter format recommended by all three major providers (OpenAI, Anthropic, Google), making it advantageous for complex prompts.

Prompt template (XML)
<role> [What perspective should AI operate from?] </role> <task> [What specifically needs to be done? Include purpose and context] </task> <context> [The situation this task sits in. Current stage, background, materials] </context> <constraints> [What should AI NOT do? Scope limits. Conditions to respect] </constraints> <steps> [The reasoning or work steps AI should follow] 1. ... 2. ... 3. ... </steps> <examples> [Examples of the desired output. 1–3] </examples> <output_format> [Shape, length, and structure of the output] </output_format>
Prompt length guide
Task ComplexityRecommended Length
Simple50–100 words (summary, factual question)
Moderate150–300 words (analysis, comparison, structured output)
Complex300–500 words (multi-step reasoning, detailed generation)
Beyond 500 wordsSplit into multiple turns
Writing principles
Use imperative verbs
Start instructions with action words."Summarize", "Compare", "Identify"
Skip politeness tokens
"Please", "thank you", and "if you don't mind" are unnecessary and can reduce performance (Dobariya & Kumar, 2025).
Use affirmative instructions
Affirmative instructions outperform negations."Use plain language" > "Don't use jargon"
Place key instructions at the edges
Put the most important instructions at the very beginning or very end of your prompt, countering the "Lost in the Middle" effect (Liu et al., 2024).
